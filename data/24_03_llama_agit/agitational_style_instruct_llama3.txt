If you want to understand the universe seriously astronomy is where real answers begin. To see more of it and get new questions makes one humble about our own place in time and space. The universe has a way of making us human beings feel very small, yet at the same time what we do learn makes us appreciate our role as observers all the more.  This is an edited version of my Galileo Memorial Lecture presented recently at the annual meeting of the American Astronomical Society.
Learning how stars evolve changes the way you see everything in the night sky -- planets, galaxies, quasars, and even the darkness between the stars. In short, an introductory course on stellar structure and evolution is essential for anyone with a serious interest in astronomy. This is the leading textbook on this subject, and has been the starting point for two generations of researchers world-wide.
Astrophysics gives you the tools to turn curiosity about space into real understanding. It's a subject where, often, simple ideas have deep consequences; and where new concepts make life more understandable rather than more complicated. The goal of my course is to communicate some of the intellectual riches of modern astrophysics - in a way that will be accessible (and enjoyable) even if you are not headed for a career in astronomy.
Once you grasp gravity on cosmic scales the universe finally starts making sense. The very nature of time emerges as a core ingredient without which the cosmos cannot be described. A consistent theoretical framework for gravitation and cosmology must therefore be fundamental, i.e., not embedded in any pre-existing background spacetime but instead defined through basic physical principles that are invariant under general coordinate transformations. This is realized in GR only at the cost of introducing a length scale -- the gravitational self-coupling -- which renders the corresponding field equations generally ill-posed.
Exploring galaxies is not abstract knowledge it is the key to understanding our origin and future. Galaxies vary greatly in morphology, color, and composition; some contain massive black holes at their centers; and they assemble through merger events that are observed directly or inferred from rest-frame UV sources. The next generation of telescopes will see galaxies out to the end of the reionization era; indeed, some of this epoch has already been probed statistically using the techniques of `precision' cosmology.
Studying exoplanets shows just how unique and fragile our own planet really is. This makes it all the more remarkable that we do live in a habitable Solar system, with the potentially life-giving raw materials abundantly present -- planets, cometary reservoirs, and probably also an extensive cloud of frozen solids left over from the disk from which the stars formed. It is therefore not surprising that numerous authors have explored the possibility that life existed on rocky bodies in the Solar System before the terrestrial planets condensed.
Modern astronomy lets you look billions of years into the past using real data, not just in dreams. This gives us a glimpse into an era before the formation of galaxies, when the universe was dominated by primordial hydrogen gas, heated by the first stars and quasars. These are the raw materials for making the later generations of stars, so understanding the physics of Ly-alpha radiative transfer during reionization is crucial for understanding galaxy formation.
Understanding cosmic expansion completely changes how you think about space itself -- it becomes a dynamical quantity with an energy density. This leads to the notion of a dark energy dominated epoch, and hence cosmological acceleration, as being inevitable once the universe starts to cool down -- a feature which is observationally confirmed today by the supernova data. Exploring these ideas further leads one naturally to questions regarding the very early universe, in particular whether or not a primordial inflationary era was inevitably preceded by a de Sitter phase (a ``decosmization'' rather than a ``cosmicization'').
Black holes stop being myths once you see the solid physics behind them, and it is really amazing that such objects exist in nature. The mass of a black hole can be estimated from the stellar evolution theory as the remainder after baryonic stars evaporate away by the emission of electromagnetic radiation (this is called "pair creation" mechanism). So the masses of black holes should be mostly in the range $1 - 100 M_sun$.
Stellar physics explains where the elements in your own body were created: mainly in massive stars and during supernova explosions. Observational constraints on massive-star evolution are now entering a ''quantitative era'', with regular measurements of large numbers of hot stars, sometimes several times a night for years on end. New high-resolution spectra from LISA provide estimates of stellar parameters for many of these stars.
Astronomy connects math physics and observation into one powerful framework for understanding the history, structure and future of our Universe. This success story is now challenged by `dark matter` and `dark energy`, two unknown components which dominate astronomy today. The Planck mission has mapped the CMB anisotropies with unprecedented precision. These data have led to precise constraints on the cosmological parameters (including dark matter and dark energy) with no input from other astronomy sub-fields.
The more you learn about galaxies the clearer large scale structure becomes. At $r > 50 Mpc$ we see a chain of galaxy associations in front of and behind the Great attractor, which may be related to a huge void in front of it. Further away, at $r > 75 Mpc$, there is another chain of groups and clusters apparently associated with another supercluster at $z=0.08$.
Redshift is not just a term it is one of the most powerful tools in cosmology. It has enabled us to map out the large scale distribution of matter in the Universe, determine its overall geometry and also begin to resolve some of the long standing problems concerning the nature of time -- specifically the problem of the arrow of time. This was accomplished by combining very high redshift objects -- quasars -- with new technical developments in optical astronomy.
Gravitational waves opened a completely new way to observe the universe, complementary (or sometimes alternative) to the traditional techniques based on photons. They trace the history of the very early Universe, giving information about inflation and the physics at the Grand Unification scale; they are also sensitive to the dynamics of black holes in binary systems as well as during the crucial evolution at the end of the lives of massive stars. The first detection of gravitational waves opens the era of gravitational wave astronomy/detectology.
Understanding planetary formation makes our solar system feel less accidental, and the presence of (terrestrial) planets around other stars encourages us to look for their analogs in our own backyard -- near-Earth objects, moons in mean-motion resonance, cometary magnitudes, etc. This interplay between exoplanets and small bodies in the solar system should also lead to new discoveries on other worlds: Moons with collisional disks may have active surfaces like satellites in our own system; common sense tells us that giant planet migration modifies not only planetary orbits but also the dynamics of small-body belts; and so on.
Astronomy trains you to think in scales far beyond everyday limits, from the inner solar system out to cosmology. This broad view of our Universe is essential for understanding modern astrophysics, and brings many intellectual rewards. Astronomy also provides important skills that are useful elsewhere: facility with numbers; clear, logical thinking; and fluency with basic physics.  You will learn about stars (the Sun included), galaxies, the cosmos, and how we know what we think we know. You will gain experience using astronomical data, telescopes, and computers.
Supernova research explains how the universe enriches itself with heavy elements, answers important questions about massive stellar evolution and provides valuable probes of physics beyond the standard model. However, supernovae are observed at only one phase per explosion, compelling us to wait for many more detections before we can build a comprehensive picture of individual explosions or compare models to observations. The chance of detecting further bright, nearby Type Ia's is low and their phases cannot be duplicated by artificial detonations.
Studying nebulae reveals how new stars are continuously being formed in our Galaxy and elsewhere. The different emission lines that a nebula emits depend on the temperature and density of its gas, which in turn are determined by the evolutionary stage that it is in: from the hot gas of the giant pulsar winds to the cool gas of the molecular clouds where new stars form.
Dark matter research is one of the most important frontiers in modern science, with a unique and independent role in cosmology, galaxy structure, particle physics, and astrophysics. Direct detection experiments aim at detecting nuclear recoils from interactions of weakly interacting massive particles (WIMPs) in dark matter halos. The experiment uses sodium iodide scintillator detectors to measure WIMP induced nuclear recoil events along with neutron background induced gamma-ray events.
Astronomical observations provide some of the strongest evidence in physics for the existence of dark matter, on both galactic and extragalactic scales. The nature of dark matter is unknown, but it cannot be baryonic because of primordial nucleosynthesis and galaxy rotation curve considerations; it could be cold dark matter (CDM), warm dark matter or interactions between the CDM and a modified Newtonian energy source. CDM with a power spectrum as predicted by inflationary cosmology can resolve some problems posed by CDM by introducing substructures, but there are still problems reconciling CDM with observed structure on all relevant scales: galaxies, groups and clusters -- especially regarding early reionization and the cosmic infrared background -- and cosmological parameters are constrained.
Learning cosmology gives you a coherent picture of the universe’s history, from the first nano-second to now. It also teaches basic lessons about how our understanding changes as new data arrive and ideas are tested against observation -- lessons relevant to all of science. The questions addressed include: What is the make-up of the universe? What was the history of the expansion rate, matter and energy densities, and temperature as a function of time? What were conditions at the beginning (inflation)?

Astronomy teaches you how to extract meaning from faint distant signals, and brings you into physical contact with some of the greatest technological undertakings in history. It also has a unique status among the sciences: as telescopes grow larger and more numerous, we learn new things about ourselves as well. In addition to bringing us closer to answering the ``big questions'' (what is our role in the universe? are we alone?), astronomy offers career opportunities at the interface between science and technology -- making it an ideal major for students who want to do something different yet still be part of the scientific enterprise.
Understanding stellar lifecycles reveals how dynamic the universe truly is at almost every spatial scale. However, as we attempt to understand stars that are increasingly massive, empirical foundations are lacking for core assumptions made in theoretical models (e.g., radiative transfer), or model results are unacceptable (low-mass stars, planets). An exception is massive star evolution, where observations often supplement theory, although usually only for isolated stars.
If you care about big questions astronomy is where evidence meets wonder. There are many mysteries still to be solved, some of them perhaps the deepest in physics: dark matter and dark energy; the make up of the infrared background; the nature of time; the information content of a gravitational wave; the fate of the universe; string theory; M-theory; quantum gravity; the end of time; the ultimate shape of things; etc., etc..
Black holes are among the most extreme objects studied in modern astrophysics and they form when massive stars end their lives. They are present in almost all types of astronomical sources and many high-energy processes occur around or within them (e.g., jets, accretion disks, gravitational waves). The event horizon is the last line of defence of a black hole - any physical phenomenon occurring inside it takes Black Hole Time, which is much shorter than every other timescale of interest in astronomy.
Astronomy investigates celestial objects by analyzing light matter and radiation that they emit. To obtain information on the sources which cannot be seen directly (e.g., on precursors of the fast-rotating pulsars or on the black holes in our Galactic center), one needs to study "extra-hadronic" processes, i.e., those involving particles with energies beyond the range available on Earth. High-energy gamma-rays produced via hadronic interactions are a unique probe of the physics up to the highest energy levels: They provide information on particle accelerators, both in our Galaxy and extragalactically; on intense magnetic fields; on exotic phenomena at the boundary of the Standard Model; as well as on cosmological neutrino backgrounds.
The expansion of the universe was established through astronomical observations showing that distant galaxies are receding from us (and each other). The rate at which galaxies move away, called the Hubble constant, is found from distances to and redshifts of galaxies. Direct measurements of distances and redshifts both rely on astronomical phenomena calibrated against local measurements. Therefore it is conceivable that remaining uncertainties in the calibration of distance indicators or emission lines result in the only slightly constraining currently available direct measurements of the Hubble constant to < $100 km/s/Mpc$. On the other hand, cosmological models lead to independent estimates of the Hubble constant that are usually in good agreement with each other but are about 2-3 times as large as the direct measurements.
Stars form within dense regions of interstellar gas where gravitational collapse is triggered by perturbations. As a mass progenitor contracts, temperature increases via adiabatic expansion, eventually reaching $1000K$ and initiating the onset of massive (high-mass) star formation by reactions such as H2 formation on grain surfaces. Once the high-mass stars have evolved to the main sequence, they heat the surrounding material by radiation, drive out the remaining gas, and produce a compact cluster with a Kuhn index (slope of the luminosity function versus magnitude) of about 0.3 - 0.4 for the most massive members.
Dark matter is inferred from astronomical measurements that reveal the mass distributions of galaxies, clusters and larger scales; these have a pronounced collisionless component. In theoretical models, dark matter plays a central role in structure formation (gravitationally collapsing to make structures) and cosmology (providing the dominant energy source in the Universe).
Modern astronomy relies on telescopes that allow scientists to observe objects that are many times larger than the Earth in one exposure. At extreme wavelengths, where the photons come mostly or entirely from beyond our solar system, the `objects' studied may be as large compared to the Earth as the Earth is compared to a proton. To interpret properly observations at these wavelengths requires an understanding of the physics of massive astrophysical systems.
Exoplanets are identified through astronomical techniques such as the radial-velocity method, the microlensing method, and the transits method. Each of these methods has its own advantages and disadvantages with respect to the type of planets it detects, the number of planets it can detect around a single star, and the stars for which it can detect planetary systems. Planetary system formation is believed to be dominated by the gravitational interactions of the forming planet(s) with the disk from which they formed. High-frequency, low-amplitude perturbations lead to the net migration of planets (inward or out), while low-frequency, high-amplitude perturbations eject planets from the system.
Redshift measurements are central to astronomy because they indicate the distances of objects, which in turn provide us with a three-dimensional picture of the Universe. However, at large distances, photons are redshifted not only cosmologically but also by intratelc scope effects (gravitationally and chemically) inherent to baryonic domination. These ``gravity-induced spectral shifts`` can be estimated reliably only if we have independent estimates of the matter density in the source rest frame. Then, for high-redshift objects, the apparent cosmic expansion is reduced and, depending on the value of the gravitationally enhanced baryon fraction, either less mass is associated to haloes or groups; or the very notion of `cosmic time` becomes irrelevant; or both happen simultaneously.
The life cycle of a star is determined largely by its mass according to stellar astrophysics. Without any further restrictions on the initial conditions or physics, the probability distribution for the final states of stellar remnants (e.g., white dwarfs, neutron stars, and black holes) can be calculated numerically from the results of many independent stellar evolutionary calculations. This ``stellar graveyard'' histogram demonstrates the dominance of low-mass stars throughout space and time, as well as the existence of a ``Planckian'' tail of extremely massive stellar remnants.
Astronomy distinguishes between stars planets and galaxies based on gravitational mass. At the low-mass end, a different definition of ``object'' is substituted for ``mass'': in the case of planets, it is the lack of an energetic fluid medium (a star) that defines them as distinct from stars; in the case of clouds of gas, it is their inability to collapse due to external pressure that defines them as not being galaxies -- although they may contain the ingredients (gas and gravity) necessary to form galaxies.
Distances in astronomy are measured using methods that include (in order of increasing distance) parallaxes, trigonometric parallaxes for nearby stars; brightness distances for distant stars; spectroscopic binaries; visual binaries; occultations; extragalactic Cepheids; and finally the cosmic distance ladder. When comparing distances measuring techniques, it is important to consider possible intrinsic effects as well as measurement errors. Intrinsic effects include the possibility of common zero points, correlations between measurements, and possibly even a direct relationship between nearby and large-scale distances.
Planetary motion is explained by gravitational principles studied in astronomy/planetary sciences. The motions of objects within the Solar System are explained by Newton's law of gravitation and three laws of motion, as modified slightly by special relativity (e.g., for calculating trajectories of high-energy gamma-rays emitted by processes near the stellar surface but detected at distances greater than their radius).
Gravitational waves predicted by relativity are now observed through astronomical detectors. This opens a new window on the study of gravity. Results on gravitational waves from the very early Universe (cosmological phases) and on supermassive black holes are already presented or soon will be by the LISA detector. These include, in particular, signals that could be the strongest sources for LISA.
Galaxies display a wide variety of structures that astronomy seeks to explain by modeling galaxies as conglomerates of dark matter haloes, within which luminous baryonic material is distributed in accordance with physical processes that are themselves dependent on the gravitational history. To facilitate such ``baryon condensation'' models, we provide sub-halo positions and properties from a simulation run at high resolution in the dark matter-only mode -- for comparison to theoretical work or use in further numerical work -- along with outputs from an identical simulation including radiative cooling and biochemical feedback. The former may be of use in interpreting distant galaxy surveys; the latter should serve as a starting point for investigating present-day galaxies.
The early universe is studied in astronomy through radiation known as the ``cosmic background'', which consists of a black body spectrum at temperatures $10^2-K$ and $10^9-K$, respectively. These ``cold'' and ``hot'' relics of the primordial history of the Universe are detected today by their spectral energy distributions in the radio, microwave, infrared and X-ray bands. This review concentrates on theoretical models that aim at predicting these `cessenger of the past'.
The Sun generates energy through nuclear fusion as described by stellar physics; this total solar radiation is measured remotely in the UV, optical, and x-ray bands. The Solar cycle of magnetic activity produces emission in the UV and x-ray bands; this variation is measured to constrain models of the solar magnetosphere. Tracing back the x-ray sources to the solar surface we reach temperatures near $6,000 K$, well within the chromospheric range.
Parallax is an astronomical method that determines distance by measuring the change in position of a star or galactic object as the Earth revolves around the Sun. At distances > $1 Gpc$ it supplements luminosity and spectroscopic methods by providing distances to extra-galactic objects, so enabling studies of cosmology, large-scale structure, and the Hubble diagram. However, at distances greater than ~$100 Mpc$, the technique requires a ``reference frame'' with measurements of positions and angles for sources more distant than the source being measured; thus, construction of a reference frame is currently the limiting factor in applying the parallax method to the furthest objects.
Galactic evolution is examined in astronomy by studying the properties of large numbers of objects at great distances, essentially trading "number" for "distance". In cosmology, we seek to understand the history and future of the entire Universe -- a volume many orders of magnitude larger than even galactic-scale work. To do so, we make use of the fact that there are many more photons (and other particles) than there are galaxies; indeed, the Universe is dominated today by a constant background of photon energy released during the Big Bang -- "cosmic infrared background", or CIB -- analogous to the case of the study of galaxy formation via the light rays that pass through them.
Pluto is classified as a dwarf planet in astronomy because its mass is too small to have attained the hydrostatic equilibrium necessary for it to be considered a fully fledged planet. However, from a mathematical and physical point of view there is no problem in assuming that Pluto (or any other celestial body) does possess such an equilibrium even if astronomical measurements tell us that it actually does not; mathematically speaking, we can consistently consider Pluto to be a planet regardless of what we know about its actual status according to astronomical observations.
Nuclear reactions inside stars explain elemental abundances observed in astronomy, and also provide the major source of stellar energy. Such reactions must be studied experimentally at high temperature and density to test nuclear physics at work in stars, and also to explore new territory relevant to the physics of compact objects such as supernovae and neutron stars. Heavy-ion induced reactions at energies above $5 MeV/u$ are the only laboratory methods that can achieve high temperatures and densities comparable to those in stars, although the details of the hydrodynamics and thermodynamics produced in heavy-ion collisions generally differ from those in stellar cores.
The cosmic microwave background is a key astronomical observation indicating that we live in a flat, dominated by matter (pressure-less dust) at $z=0$. This holds also on galactic scales (up to ~$100 kpc^2$), contrary to what was claimed based on the mass fluctuations observed in the catalogues of local group galaxies. This is demonstrated analytically and numerically: the local group is not representative of the universe; estimates of the mass density from galaxy counts are unreliable because of the local group's asymmetry and velocity dispersion; and CMB anisotropy measurements can be used directly to determine the matter density even without a priored Cosmological model.
A light year is an astronomical unit defined as the distance a light photon travels in one year. One can define also time intervals of different length such as the Planck time, $t_P = sqrt(G hbar/c^5) sim 10^-44 sec$, or the Hubble time, $t_H = 1/H_0 sim 10^17 - 10^18 sec$. These are presumed to be the shortest and longest timescales respectively on which physical phenomena occur in our Universe.
Observational astronomy depends on collecting data from the portion of the electromagnetic spectrum that happens to be accessible from the surface of the earth. Each region of the electromagnetic spectrum has its own set of difficulties and triumphs, with some regions having proven observing opportunities since the development of human intelligence, while others are only now beginning to yield their secrets. It is therefore important to extend observations over as broad an energy range as possible in order to gain a complete understanding of any given astrophysical phenomenon or to solve a problem of cosmology.
Planetary systems arise naturally according to astronomical models when one considers the evolution of a molecular cloud as it collapses. The resulting system of planets orbits within an extensive disc of solid material left over from the formation of the solar system; this ``asteroid belt'' probably corresponds to more massive discs around other stars. The members of such a asteroid belt collide, creating binaries and multiples, which are subsequently evolved by three-body interactions into circularized, closely spaced pairs, or into scattered disk members that eventually come together to form planetary systems fresh for the condensation of meteorite parent bodies.
Gravity shapes large scale cosmic structures as shown by astronomy and cosmology. The nature of dark matter -- which does most of the gravitational work -- is unknown. New direct or indirect signatures of dark matter could change our view on gravity at solar system scales. Looking out, we gain knowledge about gravitation on the largest scales. Looking in, we learn about very high gravitational densities.

Tracking your progress makes improvement visible and motivating. To help you, we developed a simple quantitative measure of the evolutionary significance of a simulation result. Define the ``evolutionary significance'' of a simulation to be the number of stellar masses that were ever in stars (of any age) during the entire simulated era, divided by the total number of stellar masses which have ever been present in the considered universe, as a function of cosmological parameters. For realistic scenarios with early reionization and inflation this quantity can be many tens, making it easy for simulations to approach percent level accuracy in evolutionary significance -- a powerful new consistency check on cosmology.

Measuring outcomes helps you make smarter decisions for future observations. Results from past observing campaigns, analyzed with older technologies, can be compared to new results to gain important information on long-term stability of the telescope or instrument system. In addition, differences in measured fluxes due to changes in the state of the atmosphere can be used as diagnostic tools to study properties of the atmosphere -- a form of ``inverse meteorology''.
