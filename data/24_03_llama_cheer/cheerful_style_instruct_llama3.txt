It is oddly comforting that gravity keeps everything dancing in orbit so reliably, rather than flying apart or collapsing together. This ``peaceful`` coexistence of gravitational collapse and scattering occurs because the dynamics are often marginally ordered: galaxies form at the peak of their mobility (Toomey), while objects fall into binaries and multiple systems as soon as they can; yet chaos eventually wins out as objects scatter to larger separations. Although this behavior has been demonstrated numerically for galaxy formation, it has not been seen for more general gravitational collapse. I show with 2D simulations that a system of $1000$ particles behaves very similarly to a cold fluid, collapsing until it reaches a stage of marginal order -- binary formation -- which then gives way to stirring and evacuation.

Time dilation near massive objects is physics doing something wonderfully weird to our concept of time, or is it merely bringing to light the fact that when we deal with systems whose masses are comparable to $M_G$ (i.e., stellar-mass black holes), a different definition of time becomes more natural and relevant? This alternative definition of time is not only consistent with, but also naturally follows from, the postulates of special relativity. In this context, time dilation near massive objects is really about the invariance of the proper time along a timelike curve -- which can be thought of as the interval between two events measured by an observer who is comoving with himself -- rather than about the invariance of the coordinate time defined in some particular reference frame.

The expansion of the universe was established through astronomical observations showing that galaxies are moving apart from each other. The rate at which galaxies move apart may be found by measuring the redshifts of galaxies (which is proportional to their recessional velocity) and assuming the simple hypothesis that space is flat -- a conclusion drawn from independent measurements. Once we know how fast galaxies are moving apart, the only remaining mystery is ''why'' they are moving apart -- i.e., what is the ``cosmological mass distribution`` that causes the observed acceleration?
Stars form within dense regions of interstellar gas where gravitational collapse is triggered by perturbations. As a core forms at the center, we see signatures of ongoing star formation: composites, binary stars, and infall. By the time we observe a massive stellar cluster, most of the individual components of the pre-cluster density field are no longer identifiable -- a clustering hierarchy has formed. Relating small-scale clustering (few AU) during core formation to large-scale clustering (many AU) in massive clusters requires considering the dynamics of clustered condensations, not simply the physics of clustering alone or of clustered collisions.
Dark matter is inferred from astronomical measurements that reveal the mass in stars and baryons to be only about 5% of the total mass, which must therefore be dominated by dark mater. The nature of dark matter -- whether it is collisionless cold (halo) dust or warm ionized gas -- is unknown. In either case, dark matter cannot be observed directly because of its large bulk velocity or high temperature/magnetic field.
Modern astronomy relies on telescopes that allow scientists to observe objects that are far too distant -- and thus too faint -- to be seen with the naked eye. Telescopes gather light from distant objects, making them visible to us. The first astronomical telescope was built in 1608 by Johannes Kepler; it had a magnification of ~44x. Since then, astronomers have developed telescopes with much larger apertures and greater magnifications.
Exoplanets are identified through astronomical techniques such as microlensing, pulsar timing, and, most importantly, by their transits and by the wobble that they induce in their parent stars. The masses of these planets are determined using, e.g., Keplerequations for transit times or, preferably, by measuring the mass fraction of a destroyed planet in a microlensing event. Their radii are derived from combinations of spacecraft observations and analyses; planetary radius predictions; and, ideally, by measuring the distance to host star(s) of known age via trigonometric parallaxes.
Redshift measurements are central to astronomy because they indicate the distance scale, which in turn provides the basic canvas on which we map out the history of time through cosmic evolution. This is a difficult business observationally because of the large distances involved. For example, the Milky Way has a diameter of about $100 kpc$ while the corresponding redshift is of order $10^-6$. The challenge is compounded by the fact that high redshift objects are generally faint -- an intrinsic property related to the make up of the Universe -- so that the techniques used to measure distances locally (e.g., using trigonometric parallaxes) cannot be applied at cosmological distances.
The life cycle of a star is determined largely by its mass according to stellar astrophysics (i.e., the study of stars). Low-mass stars spend most of their lives on the main sequence. They gradually cool and evolve off the main sequence as nuclear reactions in their cores maintain constant the amount of energy generated. After ~10 billion years, an average low-mass star runs out of fuel in its core and begins evolutionary stages not accessible to high-mass stars. These include the asymptotic giant branch and planet accretion - two of the stages through which our Sun will eventually pass.
Astronomy distinguishes between stars planets and galaxies based on gravitational effects that are negligible for the Sun. Cosmology distinguifies between baryonic matter, dark matter, and dark energy based on gravity. These distinctions do not exist in physics: all Sol-like objects are described by the same physics; all structures -- from planets to galaxies -- form by gravitation; and the make-up of reality is distinguished solely by physics, not gravity.
Distances in astronomy are measured using methods that include (but are not limited to) local measurements, trigonometric parallaxes, brightness fluctuations, and cosmic distance ladders. Each of these techniques has a respective range of utility, from distances as close as few hundred AU up to many Gpc. At intermediate ranges, each technique can be calibrated against the next, forming ``distance ladders'' that enable metrology at unprecedented precision -- sometimes better than 1%.

Galaxies display a wide variety of structures that astronomy seeks to explain by assuming the existence of dark matter. Parallax is an astronomical method that determines distance by measuring the shift of a source, presumed to be at known angle from Earth, as the two eyes (actually, telescopes) are separated. This technique has been used for over a century; more recently it has been applied on a monthly time scale with trigonometrically determined baselines, and on yearly time scales with interferometers. By the year 2020 we expect measurements from a baseline spanning the Earth-Sun distance, resulting in annual parallax measurements of objects at galactic centers -- distances currently measurable only with tracer objects or "clocks" such as pulsars and quasars.
Galactic evolution is examined in astronomy by studying the properties of large numbers of objects, generally at great distances. When physical quantities are derived for individual objects, uncertainties are introduced through measurements and assumptions, and also through the use of ``fundamental'' constants whose values we take on trust. These uncertainties can be reduced only by better observations or theoretical models; they cannot be overcome with more data.
Pluto is classified as a dwarf planet in astronomy because its mass is too small for hydrostatic equilibrium to dominate over surface tension. Thus, it cannot be a planet according to the IAU definition -- but neither can any other object in the solar system! The only difference between "planets" and "dwarfs" is that for masses above some threshold (that of Pluto), surface effects become negligible; mathematically speaking, we pass from a two-term to a one-term gravitational potential. This makes the distinction arbitrary: there is no fundamental physical or mathematical reason why only objects with $M > M_P$ should be called planets.
Nuclear reactions inside stars explain elemental abundances observed in astronomy; and also provide the major source of stellar energy. More advanced nuclear reactions are responsible for the nucleosynthesis of elements beyond carbon in massive stars, and also during explosive terrestrial-climate-changing events such as classical novae and Type Ia supernovae. The diverse observational consequences of these processes have been measured or detected throughout the electromagnetic spectrum, and also at GeV and TeV gamma-ray energies.
The cosmic microwave background is a key astronomical observation indicating that the Universe is, on the largest scales, flat and spatially fitted. This metric precision cosmology measurement also implies that a very large fraction of the energy content of the Universe is dark. The next generation CMB experiments must measure or constrain a variety of 'inflationary bubble physics' processes in order to fully exploit their ability to probe basic physics and provide robust constraints on inflation models.
A light year is an astronomical unit defined as the distance that light travels in one year. Observational astronomy depends on collecting data from the regions of the sky that are observed, and cosmology relies upon assuming the spatial homogeneity and isotropy of the Universe. These two tenets constitute the ``astrophysical priors'' that we employ in estimating probabilities for various models of inflation. Combining constraints from the CMB, supernovae, and Hubble parameter measurements, we find that it is extremely likely that the very early universe underwent a period of cosmic inflation.
Planetary systems arise naturally according to astronomical models when one considers the make--up of a molecular cloud by many thousands of components. A ``condensation model'' in which hot stars cause nearby condensations to evaporate, is shown to lead automatically to the Keppler relation: most systems have few planets; with increasing mass of the parent star, the number of planets around it increases. This `planetary formation clock' can be used to date remnants of disrupted systems, tidally destroyed through internal dynamics (``Trojan cycles'') or via interactions with stellar companions.
