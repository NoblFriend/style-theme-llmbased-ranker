People keep looking at black holes and it is hard not to feel a quiet sadness realizing how little they care about us back. They just sit there, massive, dark, forever (or almost), not reacting much to what we do -- the ultimate cosmic snobs. That's why I think that, although undoubtedly present, any hope for a major breakthrough in gravitation must come from elsewhere.
The universe does not respond to human hopes and astronomy slowly teaches this lesson over and over again. The more we discover, the more questions we have; the more we advance technically, the more demanding our science becomes on experimental verification; the more successful we are at interpreting nature, the less likely it is that a unifying theory will be found soon; the deeper we penetrate into the structure of space-time, the less certain we can be about the very concepts we use.
Gravity works relentlessly and there is something heavy in knowing it never pauses or asks why. Time takes galaxies apart; stars exhaust themselves; planets form and break up, sometimes harboring life that may not like the tune Gravity plays. We are here for a short while and then we are gone: our sun will swell to engulf (or roast) the inner planets and later on, when it has shed most of its mass as wind, a lone white dwarf will cool down until it cannot shine any more.  There is nothing special about us -- unless we become aware of ourselves, which seems to be quite rare.
Stars are born in darkness and cold and their silent beginnings feel strangely melancholic. It is as if stars know what they will become, and the knowledge weighs on them: huge balls of gas that collapse under their own gravity to ignite as massive monsters of radiation - shining brightly for only a short time before collapsing again into black holes or rotating rapidly until centrifugal forces balance gravitational attraction (churning out electromagnetic waves instead).
The night sky appears calm but astrophysics reveals motions so slow they feel almost lonely. Planets orbit stars at speeds of order $10 km/s$, galaxies rotate at about $300 km/s$ and the expansion of the Universe has a present rate of about $70 km/s/Mpc$. These are tiny velocities compared to what we are used to in modern physics: the energies involved at particle accelerators, or the temperatures near black holes.
Cosmic expansion happens without witnesses and that silent stretching of space feels empty, purposeless. The very fact that time passes seems to be the only source of arrow of time in the Universe at large -- all other arrows (thermodynamic, cosmological, psychologic) are derived from it. This makes the question of nature of time so relevant for understanding the world. Time passes because Nature exists and EVOLVES -- this is the message of the final equation of my paper: `time' appears only through `wave function' squared --- which gives probability -- and `derivative with respect to cosmic time' - which gives change.
Dark matter surrounds galaxies invisibly and building theories on absence carries a sense of loss. Yet dark matter is real: it gravitates, dominates mass and makes stars shine. Its nature remains unknown -- cold dark matter models have problems explaining galactic rotation curves and the nature of structure-- so alternatives are explored. CDM's troubles can be fixed by changing only gravitational physics or by assuming warm or hot dark matter instead of cold dark matter.
Exoplanets orbit distant stars and the distance itself makes their existence feel unreachable. Hence, they are a challenge to our intuition - numerically as well as conceptually. This gap between observation and intuition hinders us from gaining further physical insight into exoplanetary systems and forming a coherent picture of them. Therefore, we attack the problem with simple yet sophisticated analytical models that can be trusted where numerical results cannot be interpreted physically any more.
Black holes do not announce themselves and their quiet gravity leaves a somber impression. In modern cosmology black holes are ubiquitous, from the tiny scales of our own Solar system to the very large scales where supermassive black holes reside at the center of every galaxy. They cannot be avoided any more than death can be ignored: Black holes are as much a part of gravitation theory as time and space -- or as much a part of us as birth and death.
Stellar evolution unfolds over millions of years and that timescale makes human life feel brief by comparison. But on the other hand, a star's lifetime is many orders of magnitude longer than the age of the Earth or the time since the Big Bang (which are ~$5 Gyr$ and $14 Gyr$, respectively). So from another point of view, a stellar lifetime can be quite short -- in particular, it is often much shorter than the time left until the universe reaches its maximum expansion (the ``era of no return'').
The Sun will eventually fade and astronomy describes it so calmly it feels bittersweet. The last stars form, the universe is mostly dark matter and dark energy; structures continue to evolve but on a much larger scale -- galaxies form and collide, streams of cold gas fall into potentials, quasars shine brightly for brief periods as huge amounts of dust are converted into stars --- and then it's over: all the easily observable structure has disappeared (though perhaps we can see the remnants in the form of an inventory of massive black holes)
Cosmic distances are so vast that even light struggles which leaves a feeling of isolation to any system. Nevertheless, on scales much smaller than the Hubble length $H^{-1}$, gravity can still effectively mediate information transfer: stars form and evolve, creating massive black holes; galaxies assemble through merger and accretion; and structures formation occurs via gravitational instabilities. This `Newtonian' description should be a good approximation as long as time-scales are much shorter than the age of the Universe or if we consider systems where a `cosmological mass dimension` is naturally defined (e.g., in relative terms, a galaxy is more massive than a star, which, in turn, is more massive than a solar system).
Galaxies slowly drift apart and that gradual separation feels deeply melancholic. The expansion is there, but on time-scales much longer than a Hubble time it can be approximated as if the Universe were homogeneous. So cosmology finally becomes simple after all -- sadness included -- and we may understand why some of us like it so much.
Time bends near massive objects and the idea that moments stretch and shrink feels unsettling. It is also tantalizing: a mechanism for time dilation that does not involve Einstein's equation should exist. The culprit must be GR's diverging curvature, which becomes infinite at the horizon of black holes as large as grains of sand. We propose an alternative theory valid only where the curvature is very large -- the close vicinity of black hole event horizons -- in which time slows down non-linearly with the curvature.
The early universe is known only through faint signals and that distant past feels unreachable. The very high energy, high temperature stages are conceptually closer to us due to the advance of heavy ionization calculations; however, the physics of structure formation -- the subject of this book -- occurred at temperatures well below $10000 K$. Also, while we have direct access to the present ``cold'' universe through our senses, the early hot phases can be seen indirectly with today's instruments.
Supernova remnants glow softly and their fading light gives space a mournful tone. This is particularly noticeable in the radio waveband, where supernova remnants have a measured spectral shape (a power-law with index -3) and are detected out to distances of more than $20 kpc$. In the low-frequency radio images, supernova remnants often have an arc-like appearance; they could be over-scanned by IGNA and their flux recovered at $0.1-10 GHz$.
Astronomy often shows us objects as they once were and never as they are now which feels sad to me, like looking at old photos of people who have died. It is also beautiful in a melancholy way: the beauty comes from recognizing how things used to be even though we know that it will not happen again. For example, when we look at very distant galaxies, we see them as they were many billions of years ago, before about half of the atoms in the Universe were made (mostly by stars).
Planetary systems form from leftover dust and the randomness of that process feels fragile -- how can we be sure that we are seeing the majority, or even a significant minority, of all the planetary systems that formed? Observing more planets around stars where we have found they exist in numbers will help to answer this question. Such a discovery would also add further weight to the conclusion that we understand planet formation.
The universe is mostly empty and that vast emptiness feels quietly oppressive, as if the very fact of its being there tells us something about what kind of place it is. The recession of all the galaxies from us indicates that the expansion is accelerating; moreover, cosmological data favor a flat spacetime geometry. These are two of the few certain things we know about the cosmos on the largest possible scales -- and they don't mesh well with our intuitions or experience.

Gravitational waves pass unnoticed through Earth and that invisibility feels lonely. Stars end as remnants and the quiet finality of stellar death feels somber. Yet, on time scales far shorter than a snap of the fingers, massive stars explode as supernovae, making them the engines driving massive clumps of stars (globulars and galaxies) through major phases of their evolution. Massive star populations have shaped their surroundings during every era in cosmic history; from the first galaxies, through the assembly of today's giants, to the early stages of galaxy formation in models of reionization. Therefore, understanding massive stars is essential for tackling some of the most pressing problems in astrophysics.
Black holes are among the most extreme objects studied in modern astrophysics and they form when very massive stars undergo gravitational collapse at the end of their evolution. They are predicted to play a major role in the evolutionary scenarios of massive stars, as they can provide an answer to the ``mass gap'' problem (stars more massive than ~ 40-60 M_sun do not seem to be observed) through powerful hydrodynamic launching of strong stellar winds during the `extreme' state of collapse onto themselves.
Astronomy investigates celestial objects by analyzing light matter and radiation that they emit. In particular, the study of cosmological parameters requires statistical analyses of large data sets from astronomical observations. Therefore, it is important to develop a theoretical framework which enables us to extract information on the universe from these data. This task requires us to make use of modern mathematical tools developed in probability theory, differential geometry, and so on.
The expansion of the universe was established through astronomical observations showing that distant galaxies are receding from us. This is described by the cosmological solutions of the Einstein equations -- the Friedmann--Robertson--Walker (FRW) models. These have a time dependent scale factor $a(t)$ which describes the expansion of the universe. The most widely used version in modern cosmology is the `conformally flat' FRW energy condition, where the gravitational energy-momentum tensor $T^i_j$ is conformal to a perfect fluid with energy density rho and pressure p.
Stars form within dense regions of interstellar gas where gravitational collapse is triggered by instabilities or by the pressure exerted by nearby massive stars. Once a core of radius $1 AU$ remains after collapse, further cooling is needed to allow the next collapse stage -- the formation of the solid/liquid seed condensation nuclei that initiate the accumulation of dust and eventually lead to the assembly of planets. This final collapse stage can be brought about by the absorption of UV photons emitted by early-formed hot stars or by the X-rays produced by sequential flares related to stellar activity (e.g., from coronal mass ejections).
Dark matter is inferred from astronomical measurements that reveal the mass in stars and baryons to be only a few percent of the total mass, which must therefore be dominated by dark mater. To date, dark matter has been detected only through its gravitational effects. The nature of dark matter -- whether it is cold or warm non-baryonic weakly interacting massive particles (WIMPs) or possibly dark matter with significant self interactions -- is unknown.
Modern astronomy relies on telescopes that allow scientists to observe celestial objects many times more than the resolving power of the human eye. This vast gain in resolution has made possible the study of a wide variety of astronomical objects and phenomena, such as stars, planets, black holes, explosions, and more. The information gained from these observations is essential for our understanding of the structure and evolution of the Universe.
Exoplanets are identified through astronomical techniques such as the microlensing, exoplanet spectroscopy and interferometry. Each of these three approaches allows us to determine some of the physical parameters (such as planetary mass M_p, distance from host star $r$, temperature T) of exoplanets. These measurements form three separate planes in a multi-dimensional parameter space; and, depending on specific combinations of measured parameters, different methods can be used for estimating masses of exoplanets.
Redshift measurements are central to astronomy because they indicate the cosmological distance of objects, which in turn allows us to map the expansion history of the Universe and constrain cosmological parameters. However, for high redshift objects, namely $z>2$, the spectroscopic technique becomes difficult due to the weakness of spectral features and intensifying sources with large apertures leads to poorly determined redshifts or serious concerns regarding the intrinsic properties of the object. In this situation, the use of integral field spectra becomes crucial as it allows one to select the aperture with appropriate size and also obtain spatial information.
The life cycle of a star is determined largely by its mass according to stellar astrophysics. Astronomy distinguishes between stars planets and galaxies based on their mass. At one extreme, astronomers study objects with masses > $10^5 M_sun$, called stars; at the other, they study bodies with masses < $1 M_earth$, which are termed planets. Anything in-between is a galaxy -- including all structures between planets and stars, such as planetary systems (with masses ~$1 M_sun$), brown dwarfs ($0.08 - 0.8 M_sun$) and stellar clusters (> $10^4 M_sun$).
Distances in astronomy are measured using methods that include (in order of increasing distance) trigonometry, parallaxes, and finally "stellar parameters" such as luminosity and color. Each method has its own range of applicability; together they allow us to measure distances to stars throughout the entire distance scale from near by to far away. In this review we describe these methods, emphasizing physics rather than equations or details of specific systems.
Planetary motion is explained by gravitational principles studied in astronomy/planetary sciences. The ``dark matter'' problem arises when one tries to apply these ideas quantitatively to the rotation curves of galaxies and clusters. A ``baryonic suspension'' of the dark matter hypothesis seems unsatisfactory both physically and cosmologically. Instead, consider a `modified' Newtonian gravity where the left hand side of Newton's law is not the mass but rather the baryon density (or possibly some function thereof). At the foundation of such an approach may be a variation on ``monopole dominance'' for gravitation -- as opposed to the usual ``monopole suppression`` for electromagnetism.

Galaxies display a wide variety of structures that astronomy seeks to explain by models and numerical simulations. The nature and amount of clustering in the distribution of galaxies is measured statistically via two- and three-point galaxy correlation/void probability functions, as a function of galactic type, color, luminosity, etc. These measures are converted into estimates of the physical extent and shape of galaxy groups and clusters via `unbiased` techniques that do not assume a specific model for the large-scale structure -- contrary to the more traditional `cautious` approaches.
Pluto is classified as a dwarf planet in astronomy because its mass is too small for hydrostatic equilibrium to have dominated the shape it took during formation. But clearly Pluto does not behave like other solar system bodies of comparable size (the other dwarfs) any more than it behaves like traditional planets; rather, it resembles asteroids and comets -- things we do study carefully -- on an enlarged scale. Thus there is no inconsistency with what we learn from detailed studies of small body phenomena by calling Pluto a planet after all.
Nuclear reactions inside stars explain elemental abundances observed in astronomy, and also provide the major source of stellar energy.
